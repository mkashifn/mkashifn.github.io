How to Make Your Brand Appear in AI Search

A formal model of AI-search citation, with a monotonicity proof and an experimental design for measuring it

Modern answer engines don't rank pages, they retrieve evidence and generate one answer, naming zero, one, or a few brands. This paper defines AI visibility as a probability, builds a logistic evidence model of what moves that probability, proves it is monotonic in six measurable levers, and lays out a difference-in-differences design for proving, not just claiming, that an intervention worked.

Executive summary

Being findable and being citable are now different problems

Ranking #1 on a search results page used to be enough, a human would scroll to your link. An answer engine skips the page entirely: it retrieves a handful of passages, reasons over them, and writes one answer. Your brand either enters that working set and gets named, or it doesn't exist for that query.

Sections 1–3 define AI visibility as a measurable quantity. Section 4 models it as a logistic function of six evidence factors and proves the function is strictly increasing in each one (Theorem 1), worked through with a running numeric example, a brand called Acme, down to the 13 raw metrics (backlinks, referring domains, review volume, and so on) behind its scores. Section 5 then asks the practical question, given a limited budget, which lever to fix first, and proves it's the cheapest high-coefficient one, not the biggest coefficient. Section 6 adds a game-theoretic argument, a signaling model, for why independent evidence is trustworthy in the first place, and why buying it (fake reviews, link farms) doesn't work. Section 7 turns all of this from theory into a measurement protocol, including a control-group design for isolating causal effect from background noise.

Maximize the expected probability that the AI includes brand B across the query universe Q a customer actually asks.

Why this isn't keyword SEO again

The target moved from a ranked page to a synthesized answer

Keyword-era Google search was a solved adversarial game against a mostly-public, single-page scoring function: match the query's terms, accumulate backlinks, rank higher than the page next to you. AI answer engines don't rank pages at all, they retrieve a handful of trusted sources and generate one answer, so almost nothing about the old game transfers cleanly.

Matching mechanismKeyword/TF-IDF match + link-graph authority (PageRank)Semantic embedding retrieval, then LLM reasoning over what's retrieved

In the keyword era, ranking was largely a page-level optimization against a known target (match the terms, out-link the competitor). In the answer-engine era, the target is an entity's aggregate evidentiary footprint across the open web, evaluated by systems that reward exactly the things that are hard to fake, positive \(\beta_k\) on relevance, authority, corroborated evidence, coverage, clarity and freshness (Theorem 1), reinforced by the signaling result in Section 6. Tactics that used to work on a single page, keyword density, reciprocal link exchanges, thin content at scale, either do nothing here or actively work against you.

Before the equations

The same idea, five times, from a five-year-old to a proof

Every level below describes the exact same model. Nothing is dumbed down so much that it becomes wrong, each level is a legitimate compression of the one below it.

To a five-year-old

Imagine you ask your smartest friend where to eat dinner. Your friend won't recommend a restaurant they've never heard anyone else talk about, no matter how good the food secretly is. They pick the place their other friends keep mentioning.

To a curious teenager

An AI answer engine doesn't hand you ten links to click through. It reads a handful of sources it trusts, then writes one answer and names a few brands, maybe none of yours, if nobody talks about you where the AI is looking.

To a marketer

Six things push a brand into that shortlist: how relevant your content is to the question (\(R\)), how authoritative your site looks (\(A\)), how much independent evidence backs your claims (\(E\)), how much of the question space you cover (\(T\)), how consistently you're named across the web (\(S\)), and how current your facts are (\(F\)). More of any one of them, strictly, never hurts, that's Theorem 1 in Section 4.

To a data scientist

Model citation as a Bernoulli event with probability \(P_B(q) = \sigma(z_B(q))\), where \(z_B\) is a linear combination of the six factors. Because \(\partial P_B/\partial x_k = \beta_k\,\sigma(z_B)(1-\sigma(z_B)) > 0\) whenever \(\beta_k>0\), this is a logistic regression with a provable monotonicity property, not just an intuition, and Section 6's signaling model explains why the evidence coefficient is trustworthy at all.

To a mathematician

See Section 4 for Definition 2, Theorem 1 and its proof, and Corollary 1 for the concavity argument; Section 5's Proposition 2 for the budget-constrained optimal lever; and Section 6's Proposition 3 for the Spence-type separating equilibrium behind the \(E\) coefficient.

1. Defining AI visibility

A brand either appears in the answer, or it doesn't

Fix a brand \(B\) and a finite set of commercially relevant questions, the query universe:

$$Q = \{q_1, q_2, \ldots, q_n\}$$

Worked example, used throughout this paper (an illustrative composite, not an actual company; see Section 10). Let \(B=\) Acme, a project-management SaaS for creative agencies. A realistic \(Q\) might read: "best project management software for agencies," "alternatives to Asana," "how should an agency manage client approvals?" and "Acme vs Asana", the informational, commercial, comparative and brand questions a real buyer asks before purchasing. Define the appearance indicator:

$$V(B,q)=\begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } B \text{ appears in the AI answer to } q \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

The simplest visibility statistic is the sample mean of this indicator over the query universe:

Definition 1: AI Visibility Rate
$$\mathrm{AIV}(B) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} V(B, q_i)$$

If \(B\) appears in 37 of 100 representative queries, \(\mathrm{AIV}(B) = 0.37\). This is already a measurable KPI, Section 7 treats it as a statistical estimator with a confidence interval, not just a percentage.

2. Three dimensions of appearing

Mentioned, recommended, and cited are not the same event

Refine the single indicator \(V\) into three binary sub-indicators of increasing commercial value:

$$M(B,q),\ R_{\text{ec}}(B,q),\ C(B,q) \in \{0,1\}$$

these are mentioned, recommended, and cited (backed by a named source), respectively, and combine them into a weighted score:

$$S(B,q) = w_m M(B,q) + w_r R_{\text{ec}}(B,q) + w_c C(B,q), \qquad w_m+w_r+w_c=1$$

With, say, \(w_m=0.2,\ w_r=0.4,\ w_c=0.4\): a bare mention scores \(0.2\); a recommendation scores \(0.4\); a recommendation backed by a citation scores \(0.8\). This is a considerably more useful signal than a binary "we showed up", it separates being named from being endorsed. If Acme is recommended for "best project management software for agencies" and the answer cites a G2 review page, \(S(\text{Acme},q) = 0.4+0.4 = 0.8\); a bare mention with no endorsement scores only \(0.2\).

3. The objective as an expectation

Treat appearance as a probability, not a binary fact

A given brand doesn't deterministically appear or not for a given query, sampling temperature, retrieval variance, and index drift make it a random event. Define:

$$P_B(q) := \Pr(B \text{ appears in the answer} \mid q)$$

and the optimization target is the expectation of this probability over the query universe a real buyer asks:

The objective
$$\max_{B}\ \mathbb{E}_{q\sim Q}\big[P_B(q)\big]$$

In plain language: maximize the probability that the AI includes your brand, averaged over the commercially relevant questions people actually ask. Section 4 asks what \(P_B(q)\) is a function of.

4. The evidence model

Theorem 1: citation probability is monotonic in every lever

No AI search provider publishes its ranking function, and this is not a claim to have reverse engineered one. It is a stylized, testable model, a logistic function of six factors known to matter to retrieval-and-generation systems generally, built so its qualitative predictions (monotonicity, diminishing returns) hold for a wide family of coefficient values, not one guessed set of weights.

Definition 2: the six evidence factors

Let \(R,A,E,T,S,F \in [0,1]\) denote, respectively, a brand's relevance to the query, authority, independent evidence, topical coverage, semantic/entity clarity, and content freshness. Define the linear predictor and the logistic citation probability:

$$z_B(q) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 R + \beta_2 A + \beta_3 E + \beta_4 T + \beta_5 S + \beta_6 F$$ $$P_B(q) = \sigma\big(z_B(q)\big), \qquad \sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}$$
Theorem 1: monotonicity

If \(\beta_k > 0\) for every \(k=1,\ldots,6\), i.e. each factor genuinely helps and none hurts, then for every factor \(x_k \in \{R,A,E,T,S,F\}\):

$$\frac{\partial P_B}{\partial x_k} = \beta_k\,\sigma\big(z_B\big)\big(1-\sigma\big(z_B\big)\big) \;>\; 0$$

Proof. The logistic function satisfies \(\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)\big(1-\sigma(z)\big)\), which is strictly positive for every finite \(z\) since \(\sigma(z) \in (0,1)\). By the chain rule, \(\partial P_B/\partial x_k = \beta_k \cdot \sigma'(z_B)\). Since \(\beta_k > 0\) by hypothesis and \(\sigma'(z_B) > 0\) always, the product is strictly positive. \(\blacksquare\)

Every lever that genuinely carries positive weight strictly raises citation probability, none of the six factors can be safely ignored, and none can hurt you by being improved.

Corollary 1: diminishing returns, and why to diversify

Differentiating again, \(\sigma''(z) = \sigma'(z)\big(1-2\sigma(z)\big)\), which changes sign at \(\sigma(z)=\tfrac12\) (i.e. \(z=0\)). For \(z>0\), \(\sigma''(z)<0\): the marginal gain \(\sigma'(z)\) is falling as \(z\) grows. Since \(\partial^2 P_B/\partial x_k^2 = \beta_k^2\,\sigma''(z_B)\) has the same sign as \(\sigma''(z_B)\), \(P_B\) is concave in each \(x_k\) once \(z_B>0\).

$$\frac{\partial^2 P_B}{\partial x_k^2} = \beta_k^2\,\sigma'(z_B)\big(1-2\sigma(z_B)\big) \;<\; 0 \quad \text{once } z_B>0$$

A budget-constrained optimum equalizes marginal effect \(\beta_k\sigma'(z_B)\) across the six levers rather than exhausting it on whichever is already strongest. In practice: once one lever (say authority) has already pushed \(z_B\) well past zero, another unit of authority buys less citation probability than the same unit spent raising a neglected lever (say freshness), the formal reason a diversified GEO program outperforms a single-tactic one.

5. The most efficient lever

Where to start, given a limited budget

Theorem 1 says every lever helps. It doesn't say which to fix first. Real levers cost different amounts to move: unifying your own entity data is days of internal work; earning independent press coverage is months of relationship-building you don't fully control. The efficient question isn't "which \(\beta_k\) is biggest," it's "which \(\beta_k\) per dollar (or per week) is biggest."

Proposition 2: the efficient first move

Let \(c_k>0\) be the cost of a one-unit improvement to factor \(x_k\), and \(\bar c\) a fixed budget. Locally (for a small budget, around the current \(z_B\)), the first-order gain from increments \(\Delta_k \geq 0\) is:

$$\Delta P_B \;\approx\; \sigma'(z_B)\sum_{k} \beta_k \Delta_k, \qquad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_k c_k \Delta_k \leq \bar c$$

This is a fractional-knapsack allocation problem: \(\sigma'(z_B) > 0\) is a common positive scale factor, so maximizing \(\Delta P_B\) is equivalent to maximizing \(\sum_k \beta_k\Delta_k\) subject to the same budget constraint. The classical result for this linear program is that the optimum is a corner solution: spend the entire budget on the single lever with the highest benefit-to-cost ratio.

$$k^\ast = \arg\max_k\ \frac{\beta_k}{c_k}, \qquad \Delta P_B \approx \sigma'(z_B)\cdot\frac{\beta_{k^\ast}}{c_{k^\ast}}\cdot \bar c$$

Iterating this (greedily investing in whichever remaining lever has the highest \(\beta_k/c_k\), then re-ranking as costs rise per Corollary 1) is the standard greedy algorithm for fractional knapsack, provably optimal for the linearized objective at each step.

Applying Proposition 2, Acme raises \(S\) from \(0.10\) to \(0.90\) (unify "Acme" everywhere; ship Organization/Product schema) and holds every other factor at its Section 4 baseline:

$$z_B' = -1.995 - (1.2)(0.10) + (1.2)(0.90) = -1.035, \qquad P_B(q) = \sigma(-1.035) \approx 0.262$$

A single, low-cost, one-week fix moves the model's predicted citation probability from 12% to roughly 26%, before a single unit of expensive authority or evidence has been earned. This is exactly why the 90-day plan in Section 9 puts \(S\) (and the equally cheap \(F\)) in weeks 3–5, and saves the slow, expensive \(A\) and \(E\) for weeks 10–13: it is the greedy fractional-knapsack ordering from the table above, not an arbitrary sequence.

6. Evidence accumulation & signaling

Why one page you wrote is worth less than five independent ones

The authority and evidence factors above (\(A, E\)) are themselves built from many independent sources. Model each source \(i\) as contributing evidence \(e_i\), weighted by its own credibility \(w_i\), into a pooled evidence score, which crosses a threshold \(\theta\) before the system is confident enough to recommend:

$$E_B = \sum_{i=1}^{k} w_i e_i, \qquad \Pr(\text{recommend}\mid E_B) = \sigma\big(E_B - \theta\big)$$

Acme, pre-intervention, might sit at \(E_B=1.2\); an established competitor at \(E_B=7.8\); the category leader at \(E_B=15.4\), as independent, credible evidence accumulates, \(\sigma(E_B-\theta)\) rises toward 1. Treated as a graph, brand, product, category, publication, and customer nodes, connected by "mentions" and "reviews" edges, the practical goal is a dense, consistent subgraph around the brand:

Modeling heuristic, not a derived identity
$$\text{Brand strength} \ \propto\ \text{Authority} \times \text{Relevance} \times \text{Evidence density} \times \text{Consistency}$$

Unlike Theorem 1, this proportionality is a conceptual heuristic for why distributed third-party evidence compounds rather than adds, it is not proved from first principles here. It explains, informally, why 500 brand-authored blog posts move the needle far less than 20 independent ones: they add to only one node's out-degree, not to the density of the surrounding subgraph.

Proposition 3: evidence as a costly signal (Spence, 1973)

The heuristic above says independent evidence compounds. Game theory says why an AI system is right to trust it. Model each brand as having a true, unobserved quality \(\tau \in \{\tau_L,\tau_H\}\), \(\tau_H>\tau_L>0\), the AI system cannot see directly. It observes only the evidence level \(E_B\) built up above, and recommends the brand if it does so, worth \(v\) to the brand. Producing \(E_B\) isn't free: assume a cost

$$c(E_B, \tau) = \frac{E_B^2}{2\tau}$$

This satisfies the single-crossing (Spence-Mirrlees) condition, marginal cost falls as true quality rises, a genuinely good product earns reviews and press more cheaply than a mediocre one manufacturing the same volume. In the least-cost separating equilibrium, the low type produces nothing (\(E_{B,L}^\ast=0\)) and the high type produces just enough that the low type is indifferent to imitating it:

$$c(E_{B,H}^\ast, \tau_L) = v \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{(E_{B,H}^\ast)^2}{2\tau_L} = v \;\Longrightarrow\; E_{B,H}^\ast = \sqrt{2v\tau_L}$$

Check. At \(E_{B,H}^\ast\), the low type's cost to mimic is \(c(E_{B,H}^\ast,\tau_L)=v\), exactly the reward, so it's indifferent and (with any tie-break toward truth-telling) reveals itself. The genuine high type's cost is \(c(E_{B,H}^\ast,\tau_H) = v\tau_L/\tau_H < v\), strictly less than the reward, so it strictly prefers to signal. The equilibrium separates, and evidence becomes fully informative: this is the formal reason Theorem 1 is right to give \(E\) a positive, trusted \(\beta_k\).

Corollary 2, why buying fake reviews backfires. The equilibrium only holds while faking \(E_B\) is genuinely costly for the low type. Link farms, incentivized reviews and AI-written "independent" roundups all lower that cost; providers respond by investing in detection precisely because a cheaply-faked signal stops separating types and \(E\)'s trustworthiness (and effective \(\beta_k\)) collapses toward zero for everyone, not just the cheater. A cheap sanity check: cross \(E_B\) against something a low type can't cheaply fake, brand-search volume (row 13 of Section 4's audit sheet). Evidence rising while branded search stays flat is the signature of manufactured, not earned, signal.

7. Measuring it

From estimator to causal proof

7.1: Point estimate and confidence interval

Sampling \(n\) queries and counting \(x\) appearances gives the estimator \(\hat p = x/n\) for \(\mathrm{AIV}(B)\), with the usual normal-approximation interval:

$$\hat p = \frac{x}{n}, \qquad \hat p \;\pm\; z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}}$$
7.2: Before / after lift

Continuing the Acme example: with \(n=1{,}000\) prompts, \(x=120\) mentions before optimization gives \(\hat p_{\text{before}}=12\%\), matching Section 4's model prediction; \(x=230\) after gives \(\hat p_{\text{after}}=23\%\):

$$\Delta = \hat p_{\text{after}} - \hat p_{\text{before}} = 11\text{ pp}, \qquad \frac{\Delta}{\hat p_{\text{before}}} = \frac{11}{12} \approx 91.7\%\text{ relative lift}$$

Measurable, but not yet causal. AI visibility can drift for everyone as models and indices change; a single before/after comparison can't separate your intervention from that drift.

7.3: Difference-in-differences, isolating the causal effect

Acme runs the Section 5 playbook (fix \(S\) and \(F\), then \(R\) and \(T\), then \(A\) and \(E\)) on 50 topics, and holds out 50 comparable topics as an unoptimized control:

BeforeAfter
Optimized topics12%28%
Control topics11%14%
$$\widehat{\mathrm{DiD}} = (\hat p_{T,\text{after}} - \hat p_{T,\text{before}}) - (\hat p_{C,\text{after}} - \hat p_{C,\text{before}}) = (28-12)-(14-11) = \boxed{13\text{ pp}}$$

The control group's 3-point drift is background noise (new model version, seasonal demand, whatever). Subtracting it leaves 13 points attributable to the intervention, far stronger evidence than "our AI visibility went up."

7.4: A composite tracking score and competitive share

For a weekly dashboard, combine visibility, recommendation rate, citation rate, and competitive share of voice into one composite:

$$\mathrm{AIS} = V \times \mathrm{Rec} \times \mathrm{Cit} \times \mathrm{SOV}$$

where \(V=\mathrm{AIV}(B)\) from Definition 1, \(\mathrm{Rec}\) is the share of appearances that are recommendations, \(\mathrm{Cit}\) is the share that are source-backed, and \(\mathrm{SOV}_B\) is brand \(B\)'s share of all tracked brands' appearances:

$$\mathrm{SOV}_B = \frac{\text{appearances}_B}{\sum_{j} \text{appearances}_j}$$

e.g. across 100 category questions, if the four tracked brands appear 78, 63, 52 and 19 times respectively, the fourth brand's share of voice is \(19/(78+63+52+19) = 19/212 \approx 8.96\%\), a genuinely competitive metric, unlike "we rank #3 for one keyword."

7.5: Testing which factors actually predict visibility

With a panel of (query, brand, engine, date) observations and covariates \(X_1,\ldots,X_K\) (referring domains, structured-data completeness, review volume, etc.), fit:

$$\Pr(Y=1\mid X) = \sigma\Big(\beta_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_k X_k\Big), \qquad Y=1 \iff \text{brand appeared}$$

This estimates association, not causation, a positive \(\hat\beta_k\) says a factor predicts visibility, not that moving it will change your own outcome. The difference-in-differences design in §7.3, run on your own intervention, is what upgrades a correlational finding into causal evidence.

8. The six levers

What Theorem 1's factors mean in practice

R
Relevance

How closely your content matches the actual questions people ask, not the keywords you rank for. Answer-first structure and full intent-cluster coverage raise this.

A
Authority

Independent, credible sources treating you as a trustworthy reference in your category, press, analysts, high-authority reviews.

E
Evidence

The same claim about your brand, independently repeated across press, reviews, forums and docs, corroboration, not repetition on your own site.

T
Topical coverage

Breadth across the full query universe \(Q\), informational, commercial, comparative and problem-oriented questions, not just your brand name.

S
Semantic clarity

One canonical name, category and description everywhere you appear. Every naming variant adds disambiguation risk the model must resolve before it will name you.

F
Freshness

Facts (pricing, specs, positioning) kept current. Stale, contradicted claims lower a system's confidence in citing you at all.

How to research and move each one

For each lever: what to measure (feeding the audit sheet in Section 4), and what to actually do about it.

R
Relevance
Research

Pull your 30–50 question query universe \(Q\). For each, check whether a dedicated page exists and answers it in the first 100 words; score embedding similarity between your top pages and each question if you have the tooling.

Optimize

Rewrite key pages answer-first, one direct claim per heading. Build pages for every question in \(Q\) with no dedicated answer. Use buyers' own phrasing, not internal jargon.

A
Authority
Research

Pull referring domains, backlink count and a third-party domain-authority score; count Tier-1 publication mentions. Benchmark against 2–3 category leaders (as in Section 4's audit sheet).

Optimize

Earn links and mentions from independently-operated, category-relevant sources: research reports journalists cite, guest contributions, analyst briefings. Never buy links, Section 6 shows why cheap authority doesn't hold up.

E
Evidence
Research

Count independent mentions over the last 12 months, review volume across platforms, third-party comparison articles, and high-authority citations. Cross-check against brand-search volume (Section 6).

Optimize

Prompt real customers for reviews, never incentivized or fake ones. Pitch journalists and analysts genuinely newsworthy data. Get included in independent comparison content, not just your own "X vs Y" pages.

T
Topical coverage
Research

Map \(Q\)'s informational, commercial, comparative and problem-oriented questions against your content: what share has a dedicated page, and how many comparison pages exist, and who wrote them.

Optimize

Close the gaps across the full cluster, not just the head term. Build genuine "X vs Y" pages for every credible alternative. Breadth without depth doesn't help, this lever interacts with \(R\).

S
Semantic clarity
Research

Audit every place your name and description appear (own site, LinkedIn, Crunchbase, G2, Wikidata) for consistency, and check completeness of Organization/Product schema against the 10 core fields.

Optimize

Pick one canonical name, category and description. Update every owned property to match. Ship complete Organization + Product schema.org markup; claim and standardize third-party profiles.

F
Freshness
Research

Audit last-updated dates on pricing, feature and comparison pages. Flag stale or contradicted claims (old pricing, deprecated features, outdated stats).

Optimize

Put pricing and feature pages on a maintenance cadence (monthly fact-check is usually enough). Timestamp updates visibly. Retire or correct outdated claims promptly, this is the cheapest lever to hold.

9. The playbook

Turning Theorem 1 into a 90-day plan

Weeks 1–2: Baseline

Build your query universe \(Q\) (30+ real buyer questions) and measure \(\hat p_{\text{before}}\) across ChatGPT, Gemini and AI Overviews. Hold out a comparable control set of topics per §7.3.

Weeks 3–5: S & F first

Fix semantic clarity and freshness first, the highest benefit-per-cost levers per Section 5's Proposition 2. Unify naming; ship Organization/Product schema; correct stale facts.

Weeks 6–9: R & T

Raise relevance and topical coverage: rewrite key pages answer-first, one direct claim per heading, covering the full intent cluster in \(Q\) rather than the head keyword alone.

Weeks 10–13: A & E

What this model is, and isn't

  • Acme is an illustrative composite, not an actual company. Every number attached to it, its factor scores, coefficients, costs, the 12%/26%/28% figures in Sections 4, 5 and 7, and the 13-row audit sheet in Section 4 (backlinks, referring domains, review counts, and the rest), is a worked example chosen to be internally consistent and realistic in scale, not a measurement of any real business.
  • \(\sigma(z_B)\) in Section 4 is a stylized model built to be qualitatively robust, not a disclosed or reverse-engineered ranking function of any specific AI search product, those are proprietary and change frequently.
  • Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 are proved from the model's stated assumptions (\(\beta_k>0\)), which is a directional claim, "these factors don't hurt, and returns diminish", not a numeric guarantee for any one brand.   • Section 7.5's regression establishes association; only the controlled experiment in §7.3, run on your own site, supports a causal claim about your own intervention.
  • Be skeptical of anyone selling a specific "AI schema" as a guaranteed unlock, the model here argues for a portfolio of ordinary, verifiable signals (relevance, authority, evidence, coverage, clarity, freshness), not a trick.
